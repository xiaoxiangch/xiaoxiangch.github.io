Project Overview

This project analyzes customer transaction records from an Australian bike retailer to answer three questions: who the customers are, what they buy, and who generates the most value. Three source tables — transactions, customer demographics, and addresses — were merged into a unified analytical base of 19,968 records.

The analysis unfolds in four stages — customer profiling, product-behavior analysis, a cross-dimensional "who buys what" breakdown, and value segmentation via cohort retention and RFM scoring. Each stage builds on the last, converging on concrete recommendations for marketing and retention strategy.

Data Processing & Feature Engineering

  • Schema Normalization: The raw Excel file had a misaligned header row. The first row was promoted to column names and the index was reset.
  • Data Quality Filtering:
    • Removed 88 customers with unknown gender (U) — 2% of records, negligible impact
    • Removed 2 deceased customers (deceased_indicator = Y)
    • Removed all Cancelled orders, retaining only Approved transactions
  • State Standardization: Inconsistent state labels corrected — 'New South Wales' → 'NSW', 'Victoria' → 'VIC'
  • Date Conversion: product_first_sold_date was stored as an Excel serial integer. Converted via pd.to_numeric + pd.to_datetime(origin='1899-12-30'), then formatted to YYYY-MM-DD. NaN values handled gracefully.
  • Missing Value Imputation: Categorical columns (job_title, job_industry_category) filled with mode; tenure filled with mean.
  • Feature Engineering:
    • profit = list_price − standard_cost
    • margin_rate = profit / list_price
    • cohort_month = earliest transaction month per customer
    • cohort_index = months elapsed since first purchase
  • Data Merging: Three source tables joined on customer_id producing a final dataset of 19,968 records × 34 columns.

Key Insights

Across 19,968 merged records, four layers of analysis — customer profiles, product behavior, cross-dimensional breakdown, and value segmentation — converge on a single answer: Mass Customer females in NSW, working in Manufacturing and Financial Services and buying Standard product lines, are the dominant revenue group — yet High Net Worth customers earn disproportionate profit per transaction and are the true VIP target.

1 Who Are the Customers?

The customer base splits into three wealth tiers — Mass Customer (largest), Affluent Customer, and High Net Worth — with females (2,039) slightly outnumbering males (1,873) after removing 88 unknown-gender records. NSW dominates geographically and carries the highest property valuations of any state. Age, tenure, and past bike-purchase history all rise with wealth tier: High Net Worth customers are older, longer-tenured, and more engaged with the category. Manufacturing and Financial Services lead as employment industries across every tier.

2 What Do They Buy?

List price and standard cost correlate at only r = 0.55, so margin is not fixed across products. Profit is therefore driven by both volume and margin — which makes it essential to find the brand and product-class combinations with the best margin rate, not simply the highest transaction count. The online vs. in-store split further reveals distinct brand and product-class preferences, suggesting the two channels serve subtly different customers rather than the same customer shopping two ways.

3 Who Buys What?

Joining customer profiles to transaction behavior surfaces the most actionable insight of the project: the largest and most profitable segment is Mass Customer → NSW → Female → Manufacturing → Standard product line. Wealth tier also predicts what people buy — High Net Worth customers skew toward higher-class products and lean more heavily online. 4 Who Stays and Who Is Most Valuable?

Cohort analysis tracks customers from their first purchase and counts how many return in each following month. Drop-off is steepest in the first two months — those who survive past month 3 are far more likely to stay active long-term, making the early lifecycle the highest-leverage window for re-engagement. RFM scoring then ranks every customer on Recency, Frequency, and Monetary value, sorting them into High, Medium, and Low tiers. High-value customers cluster in specific states and wealth segments, and the month-2 vs. month-3 comparison shows survivors are disproportionately RFM High. Retaining early-lifecycle customers is, in effect, the same as retaining your most profitable ones.

 Key Takeaways

  • Volume and value are different targets. Mass Customer NSW females drive the most total profit through sheer volume, while High Net Worth customers earn the most profit per transaction. Strategy should separate two goals: growing the volume base and protecting the high-value minority.
  • Margin is not fixed — it varies by brand and product class. With a price-cost correlation of only r = 0.55, the highest-margin products are not necessarily the highest-volume ones. Featuring high-margin brand × class combinations in promotions lifts profitability without needing more customers.
  • Early retention is the highest-ROI intervention. Churn is sharpest in months 1–3, so an onboarding or re-engagement campaign in this window directly protects the RFM High segment — the customers most worth keeping.
